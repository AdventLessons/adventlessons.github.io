Lesson 9 · November 21, 2026

Testing the Prophets

PDFShare

Sabbath Afternoon

Read for This Week’s Study: Dan. 10:7–19; Deut. 13:1–5; Isa. 8:20; John 11:25; Acts 15:36–39; Jer. 28:9, 15–17.

Memory verse

Sunday, November 22: Role of the Supernatural

  1. List some of the physical phenomena that the prophet Daniel experienced when in vision. See Dan. 10:7–19.
  2. What does Deuteronomy 13:1–5 teach us about the prophetic gift?
  3. In some cultures, supernatural occurrences are more common than in others. Why, though, must we be very careful when seeking to know if something supernatural really is of God?

Also cited: Num. 12:6; Dan. 8:1; Isa. 1:1; Acts 9:10; Matt. 7:15.

Notes

Daniel's experience shows the overwhelming power of a heavenly vision, but supernatural signs alone cannot establish whether a message comes from God. “Therefore I was left alone, and saw this great vision, and there remained no strength in me: for my comeliness was turned in me into corruption, and I retained no strength.” (Daniel 10:8)

The ability to perform wonders is not proof of divine authority. “It was by satanic aid that Pharaoh's magicians were enabled to counterfeit the work of God.”—The Great Controversy, p. 553. “But those who look for miracles as a sign of divine guidance are in grave danger of deception.”—Selected Messages, book 2, p. 53.

Deuteronomy gives us a deeper test than what our eyes can see. “Thou shalt not hearken unto the words of that prophet, or that dreamer of dreams: for the LORD your God proveth you, to know whether ye love the LORD your God with all your heart and with all your soul.” (Deuteronomy 13:3)

When faced with extraordinary claims today, we must examine where they lead us and remain faithful to God's Word.

Monday, November 23: Harmony With the Bible

  1. Read Isaiah 8:20. What crucial point is He making?
  2. What do 2 Peter 1:19–21, Romans 15:4, and James 1:17 teach us about God and the prophetic messages that He has given over the centuries?

Also cited: Num. 23:19; Genesis 3:15.

Notes

Every claim to speak for God must agree with what He has already revealed in Scripture. “To the law and to the testimony: if they speak not according to this word, it is because there is no light in them.” (Isaiah 8:20)

This gives us a standard that does not change with popular opinion or personal experience. “While conflicting doctrines and theories abound, the law of God is the one unerring rule by which all opinions, doctrines, and theories are to be tested.”—The Great Controversy, p. 452. “Even the work of the Holy Spirit upon the heart is to be tested by the Word of God. The Spirit which inspired the Scriptures, always leads to the Scriptures.”—Selected Messages, book 1, p. 43.

Scripture carries divine authority because its message came through the work of the Holy Spirit. “For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost.” (2 Peter 1:21)

We should therefore examine every teaching by Scripture, allowing God's Word to shape what we believe.

Tuesday, November 24: The Incarnation of Christ

  1. What do the following verses tell us about Jesus? John 3:16; 2 Cor. 5:21; John 11:25; Heb. 4:14, 15; John 14:1–3.
  2. Jesus, fully God, died as our Substitute, bearing the penalty for our sins. Why is understanding this so central to our faith? What do we lose if we lose that teaching?

Also cited: 1 John 4:1–3; 1 John 4:3; Acts 4:12.

Notes

One essential test of a spiritual message is what it teaches about Jesus Christ and His coming in human flesh. “Hereby know ye the Spirit of God: Every spirit that confesseth that Jesus Christ is come in the flesh is of God” (1 John 4:2)

Christ entered our human experience, sharing the life of those He came to save. “So Christ set up His tabernacle in the midst of our human encampment. He pitched His tent by the side of the tents of men, that He might dwell among us, and make us familiar with His divine character and life.”—The Desire of Ages, p. 23.

His coming also reveals the depth of God's plan to reconcile us to Himself. “Christ was treated as we deserve, that we might be treated as He deserves. He was condemned for our sins, in which He had no share, that we might be justified by His righteousness, in which we had no share.”—The Desire of Ages, p. 25. “For he hath made him to be sin for us, who knew no sin; that we might be made the righteousness of God in him.” (2 Corinthians 5:21)

As we examine any teaching about Christ, we should hold firmly to both His true humanity and His saving work for us.

Wednesday, November 25: Positive Fruits

  1. Read Jonah 1:1–3, Acts 15:36–39, and Galatians 2:11–14. What do these verses teach us about prophets?
  2. How often have men or women of God disappointed you by their actions or character flaws? What important lesson can you learn from that experience?

Also cited: Matthew 5–7; Matt. 7:20; Matt. 7:16–20; Deut. 34:10–12; Hag. 1:12; Isa. 39:5, 6; Isa. 53:4–6; Dan. 9:15; Jer. 9:1.

Notes

Jesus taught that the results of a person's life and work help us recognise the character of their message. “Wherefore by their fruits ye shall know them.” (Matthew 7:20)

This test calls for careful examination rather than hasty judgement. “Here is a test, and all can apply it if they will. Those who really desire to know the truth will find sufficient evidence for belief.”—Testimonies for the Church, vol. 5, p. 671.

Yet God's servants were not without personal failings, as Jonah's experience makes clear. “Had the prophet obeyed unquestioningly, he would have been spared many bitter experiences, and would have been blessed abundantly. Yet in the hour of Jonah's despair the Lord did not desert him.”—Prophets and Kings, p. 266.

The disagreements among early Christian leaders also caused real harm. “This revelation of weakness on the part of those who had been respected and loved as leaders, left a most painful impression on the minds of the Gentile believers.”—The Acts of the Apostles, p. 197.

We must look for the lasting fruit of truth and obedience without expecting God's servants to have been free from human weakness.

Thursday, November 26: Fulfilled Prophecies

  1. Besides helping people know about and prepare for future events, why do prophets predict the future? See Jer. 28:9, 15–17.
  2. Read Jeremiah 18:7–10. What does this teach us, too, about conditional prophecy?
  3. Why is it important to understand the concept of conditional prophecy? How could this understanding greatly help us better grasp biblical truth?

Also cited: Deut. 18:22; Jon. 3:4; Jon. 3:10.

Notes

Fulfilled predictions provide evidence that a message comes from God, as Jeremiah explained. “The prophet which prophesieth of peace, when the word of the prophet shall come to pass, then shall the prophet be known, that the LORD hath truly sent him.” (Jeremiah 28:9)

Yet some prophetic warnings describe what will happen unless people change their ways. “If that nation, against whom I have pronounced, turn from their evil, I will repent of the evil that I thought to do unto them.” (Jeremiah 18:8)

God's response to repentance helps us understand why a threatened judgement may not take place. “But has the word of the Lord failed? Never! It should be remembered that the promises and threatenings of God are alike conditional.”—Selected Messages, book 1, p. 67.

Nineveh's experience shows how a warning can accomplish its purpose when people respond. “Their doom was averted, the God of Israel was exalted and honored throughout the heathen world, and His law was revered.”—Prophets and Kings, p. 270.

When studying prophecy today, we should consider both its fulfilment and any conditions God has placed upon it.

Friday, November 27: “Taste and See”

Also cited: Joel 2; Revelation 12:17; Ps. 34:8.

To discover more, visit https://whiteestate.org/absg/9.

Notes

The value of Ellen White's writings is best examined through personal reading, prayer and a willingness to follow the truth. “There is an evidence that is open to all,—the most highly educated, and the most illiterate,—the evidence of experience. God invites us to prove for ourselves the reality of His word, the truth of His promises.”—Steps to Christ, p. 111.

Knowing God for ourselves involves more than accepting what others have said about Him. “O taste and see that the LORD is good: blessed is the man that trusteth in him.” (Psalm 34:8) “How shall we know for ourselves God's goodness and His love? The psalmist tells us—not, hear and know, read and know, or believe and know; but—‘Taste and see that the Lord is good.’ Instead of relying upon the word of another, taste for yourself.”—Testimonies for the Church, vol. 5, p. 221.

Jesus also connected understanding God's teaching with the willingness to do His will. “If any man will do his will, he shall know of the doctrine, whether it be of God, or whether I speak of myself.” (John 7:17)

As we read and study today, we should put what we learn into practice and allow that experience to deepen our understanding.
