Lesson 7 · March 19, 1887

They Are Always Present with Us

PDFShare
  1. 1.When Hagar once fled from her mistress, where did she go? Gen. 16:6, 7.
  2. 2.Who found her there and talked with her? Verses 7-12.
  3. 3.What did Hagar say? Verse 13.
  4. 4.What may we infer from this?—That angels often see us when we do not suspect it.
  5. 5.What happened to Hagar at another time? Gen. 21:14-16.
  6. 6.Who heard the voice of her child? Verse 17.
  7. 7.How did God answer him? Verses 17, 18.
  8. 8.What may this and other passages lead us to conclude?—That God generally employs angels in answering prayers.
  9. 9.Relate another instance where an angel appeared to a servant of God. Gen. 22:9-12.
  10. 10.Where did Jacob once tarry alone all night? Gen. 28:10, 11.
  11. 11.What did he see in his dream? Verse 12.
  12. 12.Who stood above the ladder? Verse 13.
  13. 13.When Jacob awoke from his dream, what did he say? Verse 16.
  14. 14.What did he say of the sacredness of the place? Verse 17.
  15. 15.What made these solemn impressions on his mind?
  16. 16.In what place does the Lord always meet with his children? Matt. 18:20.
  17. 17.How does it seem that this must be done?—By the ministry of the angels.
  18. 18.Does not Paul, in his directions about proper conduct in meetings, refer to the presence of the angels in their assemblies? 1 Cor. 11:10.
  19. 19.If, then, these holy angels are always present where God is worshiped, how should it affect our feelings and conduct while there?
  20. 20.How did Paul recognize the oversight of angels? 1 Cor. 4:9.

Notes

We are made a spectacle.—The theater or amphitheater of the ancients was composed of an arena, or level floor, on which the combatants fought, and which was surrounded by circular seats rising above one another to a great height, and capable of containing many thousand spectators. Paul represents himself as on this arena or stage, contending with foes and destined to death. Around him and above him are an immense host of men and angels, looking on at the conflict and awaiting the issue. He is not alone or unobserved. He is made public, and the universe gazes on the struggle. Angels and men denote the universe as gazing on the conflicts and struggles of the apostles. It is a vain inquiry here whether he means good or bad angels. The expression means that he was public in his trials, and that this was exhibited to the universe: The whole verse is designed to convey the idea that God had, for wise purposes, appointed them in the sight of the universe, to pains, and trials, and persecutions, and poverty, and want, which would terminate only in their death.—Barnes, on 1 Cor. 4:9.
